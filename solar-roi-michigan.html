Michigan Solar + Storage Sizing & ROI Find the array & battery size that maximizes value against your usage — not just max production.

The peak point is where an extra panel stops paying for itself. In Michigan the trap is summer: long days make far more power than you use, and that surplus either earns a low export credit or is wasted. More panels also cover you better in the short winter days. Storage value is different: it comes from (1) riding through consecutive cloudy days and (2) capturing the midday peak for your evening load. This runs an hourly model with day-by-day clouds so both are counted.

Model assumptions
  • Daylight is a standard solar-geometry formula for your latitude — accurate within minutes.
  • Average clouds are baked into the monthly peak-sun-hour table (real Detroit-area insolation, south-facing, tilt ≈ latitude).
  • Daily clouds are added on top: a seeded, autocorrelated weather model makes realistic runs of sunny/overcast days that average to the monthly table.
  • Hourly model: each day's solar is spread over daylight hours with a cosine (noon-peak) curve; usage follows a residential load shape (evening peak). The battery charges midday surplus and discharges into evening/night load, 95% round-trip each way.
  • Peak→dark loss = kWh of your own midday solar exported while you simultaneously imported during dark hours — energy storage was too small to shift from noon to night.
  • No grid storage / banking: the grid never stores energy for you. With the net-metering toggle On, surplus is sold on the spot at the export credit; Off means draw-only and surplus is curtailed/wasted.
  • Output ceiling: if you set a cap, hourly output is hard-limited there and the excess is lost (never produced) — modeling any limit such as an undersized MPPT charge controller or inverter.
  • Net value = bill savings − annualized system cost (5% discount, 25-yr life, after tax credit). Rates flat, no escalation or time-of-use.
